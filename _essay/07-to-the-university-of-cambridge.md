## Critical Introduction to "To the University of CAMBRIDGE, in NEW-ENGLAND"
In To the University of Cambridge, in New-England, Phillis Wheatley, as an enslaved woman poet in the 17th century, addresses the privileged men in colonial New England. This poem is a call to the students of Cambridge University to seize their chance as students in their pursuit of knowledge(Wheatley line 21). Although Wheatley was educated by the family that owned her, she writes from a foreign position to the privileged students of Cambridge University; in this, she establishes her intellectual authority that overpowers her enslaved identity. Her didactic form addresses this control in the first two lines of the poem, where she says, " While an Intrinsic ardor prompts to write, the muses promise to assist my pen" where she notes her inspirations using her own will to write in this high-toned manner(Wheatley lines 1-2). The mid section addresses the students in the science fields and urges them to " scan the heights above" and excel in their learning while connecting the knowledge of science to biblical narrations of Jesus "the son of God," which reminds the students that God sees their efforts(Wheatley7-8,15-16). At the end, the poem turns back to the speaker and commands the students, saying, "An Ethiop tells you tis your greatest foe" (28). Her racial identity does not imply her inferiority; rather, it acts as a means to morally anchor her literary work. In the last stanza, the word Ethiop is italicized alongside the word Jesus in the previous stanza; this highlights a shift of meaning parallel to her religious identity(Laura Buzzard et al. Phillis Wheatley 1). Vincent Carretta argues, that despite being an enslaved woman writer;" Religion had originally given her the means and motive to exercise her voice" which acts as a bridge between the world Cambridge Students come from verses Wheatly's enslaved reality(Carretta, Phillis Wheatley, a 'Genius in Bondage'). The religious focus in the middle of the poem evokes a student's sin of neglecting their responsibility of seeking knowledge. The italicization of the word Ethiop shifts the lens to the race of the speaker to end the poem in a manner that reclaims her authority through the pen in an era where free white women were robbed of their right to attend and graduate from university. Wheatley's poem speaks to a public that exhibits this"complicated relationship with gender and misogyny in education," which continued until 1948. (An Eighty-Year Wait to Graduate: Women at Cambridge Eleanor Jones). Wheatley understands that her identity as an enslaved woman will not earn the respect of the Cambridge students; therefore, she speaks to them with the shared religious beliefs they hold. She illustrates that if the students of Cambridge do not take her advice, the "sweetness [of procrastination] turns to endless pain" where "perdition sinks the soul (Wheatley lines 29-30). This poem by Wheatley works to convey her profound level of education, warning the students of a top university to remember their Lord's blessings and perform well in their studies since privileges do not last forever. She does not ask for respect back; she simply earns it by eloquently speaking to their consciousness.

## Contextual Accompaniment to "To the University of CAMBRIDGE, in NEW-ENGLAND"
In "To the University of Cambridge, in New-England, Phillis Wheatley writes a letter to the privileged students not as a woman but as a Christian Ethiopian, which implies a silence on gender embedded in her letter. The omission of her female identity is intentional as it signifies Wheatley's limits when speaking to the privileged men from the University of Cambridge. When addressing the students, she speaks of where she comes from, " the land of errors, and Egyptian gloom" while anchoring her values by thanking God, the "father of mercy," for bringing her to "safety from those dark abodes" (Wheatley 4-6). In "Looking for Miss Phillis," Jeffers paraphrases Odell, a biographer, who fears that Wheatley "embraced whiteness as superior, and happily situated herself within a white household and elite white social, religious, and literary circles," making her an outcome of a colonial agenda(Jeffers 4). Wheatley describes her homeland as the "land of errors" where being enslaved rescued her from those "dark abodes" here; she makes this narrative of assimilation clear by taking the side of  dominant Christian society that enslaved her while brushing aside her place of origin(Wheatley 4, 6). Jeffers indicates that Wheatley's neglect of her origin could be out of fear of "being recaptured and resold into slavery"(Jeffers 4). In his essay, Jeffers highlights the paradox of Wheatley as a privileged enslaved woman while deducing that she "never could equal the privilege of eighteenth-century whiteness," presenting  her stance in society to be among the minority of the minorities (Jeffers 4). In her letter to the students of Cambridge, she urges them to "Improve your privileges while they stay," which validates this advice in her spiritual connection to the belief that no deed goes unnoticed. This irony of being depicted as a privileged enslaved woman while addressing the privileged students of Cambridge reveals Wheatley not as a preacher but as one who acts on the privileges she receives through her education (Wheatley 22).In 1868, the University of Cambridge was open for women to enrol; however, they "could still not receive their degrees" since that would make the male graduate degree less significant (Eleanor Jones, An Eighty-Year Wait to Graduate: Women at Cambridge). In 1948, eighty years later, "women were finally successful in receiving the right to earn their degrees," noting that Cambridge University came last to withhold this right. Understanding this historical context reveals Wheatley's stance in not addressing the students of Cambridge with her identity as a woman and, in return, choosing the orientalist narrative that depicts her strong faith and dedication to the Christian school of thought as a common ground between the free and enslaved.
