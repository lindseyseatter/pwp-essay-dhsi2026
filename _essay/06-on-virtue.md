## Critical Introduction to "On Virtue"  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Phillis Wheatley Peters’ “On Virtue” appears in her 1773 poetry collection Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral. The poem explores virtue as an ideal that the speaker wishes to understand, develop, and follow throughout life. Rather than presenting virtue as an abstract quality alone, Wheatley personifies it as a feminine, heavenly presence that offers guidance and encouragement to the human soul. Through symbolism, personification, and Christian religious imagery, Wheatley explores the relationship between wisdom, moral discipline, and spiritual fulfilment. Readers should approach the poem with an understanding of its religious language and its emphasis on the continuous pursuit of goodness.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The opening addresses virtue as a “bright jewel” (line 1), using symbolism to represent something precious and valuable that the speaker strives to attain. However, although the speaker desires to understand virtue, this introduces a tension between the desire for moral improvement and the limitations of human knowledge. The speaker acknowledges these limitations but does not allow them to lead to discouragement, stating, “But, O my soul, sink not into despair” (line 6). Harvey and Vine also explore this idea of the growing and incorporating religious and morality shaping texts in 18th Century Britain and Colonial America. Further considering, “On Virtue” is one of Wheatley's famous texts she incorporates the idea of religion to grow and appeal to this larger audience and develop a trust and common intrest between the two.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Moreover, through religious imagery, Wheatley suggests that goodness is not entirely unreachable but can guide and support those who actively seek it. Wheatley further develops this idea through personification, presenting virtue as a feminine figure and an “Auspicious queen” with “heav’nly pinions spread” (line 11). The description of a queen gives virtue authority and importance, while her heavenly wings associate her with the divine. She is accompanied by “celestial Chastity” (line 12), connecting virtue to moral purity and self-discipline. Religious imagery is essential to understanding the poem’s central message. References to heaven, cherubs, and “endless life and bliss” (line 17) connect the pursuit of virtue to Christian morality and spiritual salvation. Wheatley contrasts these eternal rewards with the “false joys of time” (line 16), suggesting that temporary worldly pleasures cannot provide the same fulfilment as a virtuous life. In the final stanza, the speaker questions whether virtue should be called “Greatness, or Goodness” (line 18), suggesting that ordinary language may be insufficient to describe its significance. The request for “a better strain, a nobler lay” (line 19) also demonstrates the speaker’s desire to express virtue through more elevated poetic language.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Wheatley Peters’ position as an enslaved African woman publishing poetry in eighteenth-century America provides important historical context. Her 1773 publication was a significant literary achievement during a period when Black intellectual and literary abilities were frequently questioned. Although “On Virtue” is not an explicit political statement, it demonstrates her intellectual authority and engagement with religious and literary traditions at the time. Ultimately, the poem presents virtue as a precious and guiding presence that requires patience, wisdom, and spiritual commitment. Within the wider collection, it reflects Wheatley’s interest in Christian morality and the relationship between human character and spiritual fulfilment.

## Contextual Accompaniment to "On Virtue"  
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The meaning of virtue in Phillis Wheatley Peters’ “On Virtue” can be better understood by considering the moral and religious expectations of eighteenth-century society. During this period, morality was strongly connected to Christianity, particularly the belief that individuals should demonstrate goodness through their behaviour, self-discipline, religious devotion, and relationship with God. Living a virtuous life was not simply about following social expectations but also about developing a morally responsible character and preparing for eternal salvation. These beliefs are reflected throughout Wheatley’s poem, particularly through references to “Chastity,” “endless life and bliss,” and the heavenly presence of virtue. The poem suggests that living morally requires continuous effort, guidance, and an understanding that earthly pleasures are temporary compared to spiritual rewards.
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The title “On Virtue” is significant because virtue was understood through different moral and social expectations, particularly concerning gender. During the eighteenth century, women were often associated with qualities such as chastity, modesty, purity, and religious devotion. These qualities were considered important characteristics of a respectable woman and influenced how female morality was judged within society. Wheatley reflects some of these expectations through her personification of virtue as an “Auspicious queen” who leads “celestial Chastity” (lines 11–12). By presenting virtue as a feminine figure, Wheatley draws upon the association between womanhood and moral purity. This representation gives femininity a position of spiritual importance and authority. The speaker’s desire to be guided by virtue also suggests that morality is something individuals must consciously pursue, regardless of their social position. Christianity also shaped how individuals understood the relationship between morality and everyday life. Religious teachings encouraged people to resist temptation, avoid excessive attachment to worldly pleasures, and prioritize spiritual goodness. Wheatley expresses this distinction when she asks virtue, “O leave me not to the false joys of time\!” (line 16). The word “false” suggests that temporary pleasures can distract individuals from developing a meaningful and morally responsible life. 
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;However, understanding morality in eighteenth-century America also requires recognizing the contradictions between Christian principles and the social realities of the period. Wheatley was an enslaved African woman writing within a society that promoted Christian morality while simultaneously permitting slavery and racial inequality. Christianity emphasized the importance of spiritual goodness and the value of one’s relationship with God, yet these principles were not consistently extended to the treatment of enslaved people. Micheletti and Bryc eat al shine light in their article on the contradictory sexism in the belief that enslaved women must be pious and obedient whilst, the enslavers would rape, segregate etc. committing larger sins.This historical contradiction provides another way to approach the poem. Her writing demonstrates her participation in the religious and intellectual traditions of her time despite the inequalities surrounding her. 
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Ultimately, Wheatley’s understanding of virtue encourages readers to consider morality as something deeper than outward respectability or social status. Through Christian imagery and the personification of virtue, she presents goodness as an ongoing commitment to wisdom, self-discipline, and spiritual growth. The historical context makes this message particularly meaningful because it invites readers to consider whether the moral values promoted by society were genuinely reflected in how people treated one another.
