## Critical Introduction to "On Recollection"
In her book on American lyric, Virginia Jackson contemplates Phillis Wheatley Peters' memory by posing a few questions: "Do Wheatley's poems speak for themselves? What do we mean when we say a poem 'speaks'? Do we mean that the poet expresses herself through the poem? Do we mean that the poem speaks for her? Or do we mean that the poem achieves a voice of its own?" (108). There is plenty of criticism on Wheatley Peters' poem "On Recollection" that is driven by these questions of speaker, voice, and subject. The speaker -- whomever it may be -- invokes Mneme, the Muse of memory, to aid her in recollecting "acts of long departed years" and undefined "horrid crime[s]" of the past (5, 27). There is an inclination among scholars to delineate Phillis herself as the speaker and that slavery is the "horrid crime" she is calling on Mneme to retrieve, and these "stakes of who speaks and why" is described by Jackson as "very high indeed" (112).

In one exploration of Wheatley Peters' poetry, Devona Mallory intends to analyze the presence of a goddess-mother in three poems, including "On Recollection." The main issue with the article is that the foundation for which her analysis sits is established through popular, imagined narratives about Wheatley Peters. One quote from Mallory exemplifies the kind of presumptuous work that Wheatley Peters is frequently subjected to:
<blockquote>Imagine Wheatley at seven years old, standing on a slave block, mostly nude, probably shivering with shock and/or cold. What thoughts were going through her head? How traumatic is must have been to her, losing her mother not to death but to slavery. It must have been another blow to realize she would never see her mother again. Wheatley eventually learns that one way to cope is by composing poetry. (21)</blockquote>
By basing analysis on assumptions about experiences Wheatley Peters "must have" had, regardless of how true any of the specifics could be, the arguments that follow are fundamentally weakened. Furthermore, there is something intensely voyeuristic about the call to "imagine Wheatley" in such a vulnerable position. She is poised here as an object for study, an oddity through which we can come to understand the extraordinary circumstances that she is in. It gives little room to conceive of her with any meaningful focus on her individual personhood. In so frequently repeating this story of her arrival to Boston -- and certainly by inviting imagination of the scene -- the images conjured are only reinforced as central to her memory.

Contemplating the potential subjects of "On Recollection," John C. Shields asks "indeed what would an asthmatic, benign teenager closely supervised by her white owners know of 'the worst tortures'?" (47). Like with Mallory, Wheatley Peters is afforded little room for complexity. The question concludes that slavery is the only pertinent subject she could be writing about but Shields does so with very little textual evidence and while granting her no authority over her mental and artistic capabilities. Jackson questions Shield's methods of analysis, arguing that he focuses too closely on "giving in to such imaginings" that pervade Wheatley Peters scholarship and "makes the objects of shame and mourning that remain both overdetermined and indeterminate in the poem into one person's historical experience" (118-119). Once again, Wheatley Peters is meant to be the figurehead for an imagined, collective slave experience who, despite the circumstances, becomes exceptional through her poetic abilities.

We cannot take for granted the little information we have on Wheatley Peters' life. Jackson calls the oft repeated narrative of her arrival to Boston a "deceptively basic opening statement" that stems from Margaretta Matilda Odell's questionable memoir that has provided many pervasive narratives about Wheatley Peters (103). But within Jackson's book we find a remark that Odell has been "confirmed" as related to Susanna Wheatley, as if that fact would determine Odell's relevancy towards Wheatley Peters' life or prove that she is truly even wrote it (124). Honorée Fanonne Jeffers, whose research centers Wheatley Peters' life and the convoluted string of misconceptions and misinformation surrounding her biography, found that Odell may not have been the author of the biography at all. Jeffers discovered two biographies on Wheatley Peters had been published in Boston in 1834: one written by a male author, B.B. Thatcher, and the other by an unnamed "relative of the Mistress of Phillis" (102). She theorizes that perhaps "adopt[ing]  female _nom de plume_" and boosting authority by presenting the author with direct familial connection to the Wheatley family took place "in order to sell more books for a higher price" (105). If Wheatley Peters' memory can tell us anything it is that perceived truths, even from sources considered invaluable, should always be open to interrogation. "The stakes of who speaks and why" applies not only to her poetry, but the sources we use as we glean the story of her life (Jackson 112).

To conclude, I would like to return to one of Jackson's questions: "Do Wheatley's poems speak for themselves?" (108). In articles like Mallory's or Shields', there are many assumptions of subject in Wheatley Peters' poems with little strong textual evidence to support it. Shields' idea that Phillis is the central subject of "On Recollection" is only weak insofar as the lack of evidence from the poem he provides. Indeed, the poem itself does contain evidence for this reading, including personal descriptors and the use of pronouns in the opening stanza. The opening lines of the poem start a direct dialogue between Muse and speaker, first with a short statement of "Mneme begin," with a full stop (1). The speaker continues by imploring her and the other "sacred nine" to "Inspire ... / Your vent'rous _Afric_" to recall the memories she looks to recover. The muses are instantly identified as "sacred", and the speaker as "Your vent'rous _Afric_," establishing who is speaking to whom and their standing with one another (2). The speaker then presents what she offers to Mneme: "I trace thy spring" and "I thy glories sing" (3-4). The following line is directed towards Mneme before the speaker's individual position is expanded to include a collective of people: "The acts of long departed years, _by thee_ / Recover'd, in due order rang'd _we see_" (5-6; emphasis added). Who is speaking, who is being spoken to, and who is included as a subject is pivotal to the opening stanza; Wheatley Peters uses every bit of space available to her in the  stanza to establish the characteristics, positions, and motivations of those mentioned in within. With these details in place, she can continue her examination of the nature of memory on a solid foundation. Words like our, her, she, and we are used throughout the following stanzas, but the speaker only returns to the personal "mine" in the fourth. The first line of this stanza states that "Now eighteen years" have passed since the events of her lost memories occurred or began, the age which Wheatley Peters was when she composed the first version of the poem (31). Continuing to reflect on these memories, she "In Recollection see[s] them fresh return, / And sure 'tis _mine_ to be asham'd, and mourn" (35-36; emphasis added). That Wheatley Peters returns to a personal perspective with the use of "mine" in the same stanza containing a direct link to her own life lends authority to the question of whether the speaker is the poet herself.

## Contextual Accompaniment to "On Recollection"
The publication of _Poems on Various Subjects_ was certainly a significant feat. Dwight A. McBride describes the achievement of publishing, out of all mediums, "an ample book of more than a hundred pages" as a crucial piece of context in Wheatley Peters' success. That the book was "comparatively expensive" to other mediums and contained "an elaborate frontispiece and widely spaced type ... lent distinction to _Poems on Various Subjects_ as an item of heightened aesthetic value" (373-374). Scholars will often note that Wheatley Peters failed to get a second volume of poetry published in the period between her emancipation and her death around a decade later (although Jeffers manages to find evidence that her husband attempted to get it published after her death (98-99)). The very first page of the Penguin edition of her Complete Writings -- prior to the title page, bibliographic information, and table of contents -- is a short biography that ends equally succinct as it is bleak: "Having failed to find a publisher for her proposed second volume of poems, Phillis Wheatley Peters died in poverty in Boston on December 5, 1784. She was buried in an unmarked grave with her youngest child on December 8" (Carretta). For the new reader, this could be the very first impression of Wheatley Peters' final years. But "On Recollection" is only one example of her success in the other avenues through which her poems were disseminated.

"Recollection" was originally published in the March 1772 edition of _London Magazine_, a monthly publication of political writings, current events, and popular discourse. The frontispiece of this volume contains an illustration of _London Magazine_, represented by a winged woman, "Unmasking the Times", who carries with it subjects such as "Popularity," "Corruption," "Bankruptcies," "Fraud," "Bigotry," and "Patrotism." The poem, published under "Political Essays," is prefaced by a letter to the magazine that explains the origins of the poem's composition:

<br> _To the_ AUTHOR _of the_ LONDON MAGAZINE [March 1772].

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;Boston, in New-England, Jan. 1, 1772.

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;SIR,

<br>As your Magazine is a proper repository for any thing valuable or curious, I hope you will excuse the communicating the following by one of your subscribers.

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;There is in this town a young _Negro woman_, who left _her_ country at ten years of age, and has been in _this_ eight years. She is a compleat sempstress, an accomplished mistress of her pen, and discovers a most surprising genius. Some of her productions have seen the light, among which is a poem on the death of the Rev. Mr. George Whitefield.--The following was occasioned by her being in company with some young ladies of family, when one of them said she did not remember, among all the poetical pieces she had seen, ever to have met with a poem upon RECOLLECTION. The _African_ (so let me call her, for so in fact she is) took the hint, went home to her master's, and soon sent what follows.

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;"MADAM,

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;Agreeable to your proposing _Recollection_ as a subject proper for me to write upon, I enclose these few thoughts upon it; and, as you was the first person who mentioned it, I thought none more proper to dedicate it to; and, if it meets with your approbation, the poem is honoured, and the authoress satisfied. I am, Madam,

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Your very humble servant,

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;PHILLIS."

<br>It was known that prior to _Poems_ Wheatley Peters' work had already been published "as pamphlets or broadsides that had appeared in more than a dozen distinguished periodicals from both sides of the Atlantic" (McBride 375). The composer of the letter presenting "Recollection" mentions that "some of her productions have seen the light, among which is a poem on the death of Rev. Mr. George Whitefield," promoting her as an established writer. The following is the original version of the poem as it was published in _London Magazine_.

#### RECOLLECTION.
_To Miss A--M--, humbly inscribed by the Authoress._

<br>MNEME, begin; inspire, ye sacred Nine!
<br>Your vent'rous _Afric_ in the deep design.
<br>Do ye rekindle the coelestial fire,
<br>Ye god-like powers! the glowing thoughts inspire,
<br>_Immortal Pow'r!_ I trace thy sacred spring,
<br>Assist my strains, while I _thy_ glories sing.
<br>By _thee_, past acts of many thousand years,
<br>Rang'd in due order, to the mind appears;
<br>The _long-forgot_ thy gentle hand conveys,
<br>_Returns_, and soft upon the fancy plays.
<br>Calm, in the visions of the night he pours
<br>Th'exhaustless treasures of his secret stores.
<br>Swift from above he wings his downy flight
<br>Thro' _Phoebe's_ realm, fair regent of the night.
<br>Thence to the raptur'd poet gives his aid,
<br>Dwells in his heart, or hovers round his head;
<br>To give instruction to the lab'ring mind,
<br>Diffusing light coelestial and refin'd.
<br>Still he persues, unweary'd in the race,
<br>And wraps his sense in the pleasing maze.
<br>The Heav'nly Phantom _points_ the actions done
<br>In the past worlds, and tribes beneath the sun.
<br>He, from his throne in ev'ry human breast,
<br>Has _vice_ condemn'd, and ev'ry _virtue_ bless'd.
<br>Sweet are the sounds in which thy words we hear,
<br>Coelestial musick to the ravish'd ear.
<br>We hear thy voice, resounding o'er the plains,
<br>Excelling Maro's sweet Menellian strains.
<br>But awful _Thou!_ to the perfidious race,
<br>Who scorn thy warnings, nor the good embrace;
<br>By _Thee_ unveil'd, the horrid crime appears,
<br>_Thy_ mightly hand redoubled fury bears;
<br>The time mis-spent augments their hell of woes,
<br>While through each breast the dire contagion flows.
<br>Now turn and leave the rude ungraceful scene,
<br>And paint fair Virtue in immortal green.
<br>For ever flourish in the glowing veins,
<br>For ever flourish in poetick strains.
<br>Be _Thy_ employ to guide my early days, 
<br>And _Thine_ the tribute of my youthful lays.

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;Now *_eighteen years_ their destin'd course have run,
<br>In due succession, round the central sun;
<br>How did each folly unregarded pass!
<br>But sure 'tis graven on eternal brass!
<br>To _recollect_, inglorious I return;
<br>'Tis mine past follies and past crimes to mourn.
<br>The _virtue_, ah! unequal to the _vice_,
<br>Will scarce afford small reason to rejoice.

<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;Such, RECOLLECTION! is thy pow'r, high-thron'd
<br>In ev'ry breast of mortals, ever own'd.
<br>This wretch, who dar'd the vengeance of the skies,
<br>At last awakes with horror and surprise.
<br>By _Thee_ alarm'd, he sees impending fate,
<br>How howls in anguish, and repents too late.
<br>But oft _thy_ kindness moves with timely fear
<br>The furious rebel in his mad career.
<br>Thrice bless'd the man, who in _thy_ sacred shrine
<br>Improves the REFUGE from the wrath divine.

<br>*Her age.
