## Critical Introduction to "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8"
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;A paraphrase of the _Old Testament_ book for which it is named, Phillis Wheatley Peters’s poem "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" retells the prophecy detailed in "Isaiah's" sixty-third chapter, comparing the “Almighty Savior['s]” work in a wine press and a righteous vengeance he enacts across a battlefield (Wheatley Peters line 9). Wheatley Peters's musings on the Christian faith are the focus of some of her most well known and, occasionally, controversial poems, such as “On being brought from Africa to America,” also included in Wheatley Peters's _Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral._ The poem has been the subject of much criticism by poets and scholars alike (Jeffers 90, Thorn 121), but has also been praised for the complicated rhetorical work that Wheatley Peters accomplishes in its few lines. To better understand the way the author utilizes biblical allusions in "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8," we can look to a reading of this poem by William J. Scheick, which illuminates Wheatley Peters's messaging, as well as the implications it has on her other biblically centred work.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Scheick identifies that the ambiguity created by the use of punctuation in the final lines of “On being brought from Africa to America” “refutes the notion, held by many of Wheatley's contemporaries, that Cain, marked by God, is the progenitor of the black race only… [instating] both Christians and [Black Americans] as the mutual offspring of Cain who are subject to refinement by divine grace“ establishing an equality between humanity in their Christian faith (Scheick 136). That this aligns with our understanding of her politics from the broader collection of both published and unpublished poetry, as well as letters and newspaper publications, gives credence to this reading (Carretta 176). For those looking to better understand "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8," Scheick's analysis establishes Wheatley Peters's use of familiar biblical imagery and theology to argue her own politics and values, specifically of an innate equality of Black and white Christians. Understanding this reading of Wheatley Peter's messaging present in the fifth poem of her collection creates a framework for interpreting the significantly more metaphorically veiled "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" tucked further into the collection. 

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Readers of “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8” will likely quickly identify the references to the atonement of Christ, the “king or mighty God” whose “soul [he] sacrific'd with willing hand” so mankind could be redeemed (Wheatley Peters lines 1, 16). What defines the poem's specific depiction of Christ's sacrifice is the emphasis on isolation, the performance of labour ‘forsaken’ by all but himself, and the physical cost of this labour, which all create a Christ figure coded in the same language that evokes the experience of enslavement (lines 7-11). The wine-press becomes pivotal in communicating this imagery. In comparison to its source material, “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8” obscures the gore found in these verses by focusing its attention on the symbolic wine itself. In the King James Version translation of _The Bible,_ it is stated explicitly that the dye staining the subject's “apparel” is the blood of the enemies of the Lord (King James Version, Isaiah 63.2-3). On the other hand, while not lacking violence, Wheatley Peters’s poem depicts “his purple vesture” as stained by the wine that “bled, and pour'd the gushing purple round” (Wheatley Peters line 8). The symbolic connection between wine and blood would be a known comparison for Wheatley Peters' Christian audience, familiar with the ritual of communion. What the change accomplishes is bringing the reader's attention to the cost of the physical labour intrinsically tied to the production of the wine, a commodity. Using personifying language that describes the press as ‘bleeding’ and ‘groaning’ depicts Christ as bearing these physical costs of this production just as he bore the sins of the world (lines 7-8). 

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;This association that Wheatley Peters develops has further theological implications. Take for example the following couplet from the second stanza: “‘For man's release [the Saviour] sustain'd the pond'rous load,/’For man the wrath of an immortal God’” (line 13-14). In the context of the labour imagery, the term ‘release’ holds a double meaning, connecting the release of humanity from sin and release from enslavement, intertwining Christ's sacrificial atonement (the keystone of Christian theology) with an argument about the morality of slavery. Following this, a reader can make assumptions about who is numbered among the “haughty foes” receiving his “vengeance” (line 22). 

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Just as Wheatley Peters demonstrated in “On being brought from Africa to America,” the poet adeptly draws on well-known and recognizable Christian imagery in "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" and reframes it, arguing the incongruency of slavery within Christianity and imbuing a depiction of the abolitionist cause with the energy and imperative of a righteous, holy battle.

## ISAIAH LXIII. 1-8.

SAY, heav’nly muse, what king or mighty God,
  That moves sublime from Idumea’s road?
  In Bosrah’s dies, with martial glories join’d,
  His purple vesture waves upon the wind.
  Why thus enrob’d delights he to appear
  In the dread image of the Pow’r of war?
    Compres’d in wrath the swelling wine-press groan’d,
  It bled, and pour’d the gushing purple round.
    “Mine was the act,” th’ Almighty Saviour said,
  And shook the dazzling glories of his head,
  “When all forsook I trod the press alone,
  “And conquer’d by omnipotence my own;
  “For man’s release sustain’d the pond’rous load,
  “For man the wrath of an immortal God:
  “To execute th’ Eternal’s dread command
  “My soul I sacrific’d with willing hand;
  “Sinless I stood before the avenging frown,
  “Atoning thus for vices not my own.”
     His eye the ample field of battle round
  Survey’d, but no created succours found;
  His own omnipotence sustain’d the right,
  His vengeance sunk the haughty foes in night;
  Beneath his feet the prostrate troops were spread,
  And round him lay the dying, and the dead.
    Great God, what light’ning flashes from thine eyes?
  What pow’r withstands if thou indignant rise?
    Against thy Zion though her foes may rage,
  And all their cunning, all their strength engage,
  Yet she serenely on thy bosom lies,
  Smiles at their arts, and all their force defies.

## Contextual Accompaniment to "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8"
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In the final stanza of Phillis Wheatley Peters’s “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8,” the author diverges noticeably from its namesake to describe the figure of ‘Zion’ against whom the enemies of God ‘rage’ to no avail (lines 27-30). While the literary legacy of this biblical city is long and complex (Zion, Britannica), for Wheatley Peters’s poetic ends the metaphorical Zion of this poem is in the tradition of depicting the city as a prophesized utopia. The imminence of Wheatley Peters's vision for the future, as well as the moral alignment of abolitionism and Christianity is communicated in the way that the personified Zion “serenely on [the Savior's] bosom lies,/Smile[s] at [her foes] arts, and all their force defies” (lines 29-30). In the context of the abolitionist messaging of the text, Zion is representative of a society where the practice of slavery is abolished. Looking at the author's contemporary moment and context, we can see the public discourse and fast changing attitudes likely  informed the author's hopeful depiction of a near and inevitable emancipated future.

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;In pursuing the publication of her poetry in England when she did, Wheatley Peters’s freedom was within possibility. At the time of her voyage to London, precedent had been established for enslaved persons to be freed under certain circumstances: In British courts in 1772, the year before Wheatley took her voyage across the Atlantic to London, in [_Somerset v. Stewart_](https://slaverylawpower.org/chapters/revolutionary-atlantics/somerset-v-steuart/) “Lord Mansfeild ruled that James Somerset, an enslaved person brought from Massachusetts to England in 1769, could not be forcibly removed from England against his will,” effectively ending his enslavement (Erkkila 360). With this context, Britain had the potential to be “the site of [Wheatley Peters's] anticipated emancipation”, a manifestation of the version of ‘Zion’ detailed in “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8” for herself and a handful of other enslaved persons (Carretta 155). Betsy Erkkila reads another of Wheatley's poems featured in her published collection, “A Farewel to America,” under the assumption of Wheatley Peter's considering a plan to obtain freedom in England, further supported by the notable figures in Britain's abolitionist movement Wheatley Peters met during her time in London (Erkkila 360). A similar reading might be made of the depiction of the femininely coded figure of Zion in the final stanza of “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8.”

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Of course, Wheatley Peters did not take her freedom in London, instead being freed a month after returning to the Wheatley's home in Massachusetts, and quickly setting herself to the work of funding her independence through the sales of her book, ensuring legitimacy by signing copies, knowing the threat piracy would pose to her sales (Carretta 156). What might have contributed to her decision to return from London was her prediction that abolition was fast approaching in America. As tensions between the British Empire and its colonial subjects in America intensified, “[t]he abolitionist movement was increasingly conflated in the northern colonies with opposition to imperial rule” and Phillis Wheatley Peters's residence in Boston would have served as an ideal place to witness and engage the growing momentum of both movements (Carreta 155). Aside from residing on King Street, “the seat of Massachusetts government,” the church where Wheatley Peters was baptized, the Old South Church, was the site where Bostonians members would gather in response to several notable events in the lead up to the American Revolution, like the Boston Massacre of 1770 and “the destruction of tea in Boston Harbor… later known as the ‘Boston Tea Party’” (Erkkila 353).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;The depiction of the “dread image of the Pow'r of war” in “Isaiah lxiii. 1-8” (Wheatley Peters line 6), a poem written to mimic a biblical prophecy of a battle preceding a utopia, would align with this belief that the success of the colonies in the Revolutionary War would bring with it emancipation. However, to say that Wheatley Peters took this outcome for granted would be inaccurate. Her political engagement and abolitionist advocacy only became more explicit and forthright after her emancipation, including a letter to Reverend Samson Occum, a “popular Mohegan minister” and advocate for both Indigenous and Black Americans, that “was published in the Connecticut Gazette and then widely circulated in New England newspapers,” in which she asserted the innate desire for freedom in all human beings (Erkkila 364-365). It is clear to see that Wheatley Peters was hopeful that the freedom she imagined in the last lines of "Isaiah lxiii. 1-8" would come to pass, and that her poetry was an integral part of her efforts to realize it, crafting in her art the image of a better world that gave her the momentum to advocate for it existence.
